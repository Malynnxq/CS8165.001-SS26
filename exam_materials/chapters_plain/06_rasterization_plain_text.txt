Lecture six is about Rasterization.

The central idea is this. Rasterization converts continuous projected geometry into discrete fragment candidates and interpolated per fragment attributes. The useful mental chain is projected primitive, then sample coverage, then fragment generation, then attribute interpolation, then fragment tests. This chain matters because every later question in the chapter becomes easier when you know what enters a step, what changes inside the step, and what leaves the step.

Before the details, keep the full rendering story in mind. Scene data is prepared, transformed, projected, converted to fragments, tested, shaded or combined, and finally written into a framebuffer. This chapter is one focused part of that larger story.

The first idea in this lecture is Line Rasterization.

A mathematical line has infinitely many points, but a raster display has a finite grid. Line rasterization decides which grid cells or samples best approximate the ideal line. The algorithm must balance accuracy, speed, and consistency.

Incremental algorithms avoid recomputing expensive formulas for every pixel. The important idea is to step along one axis and update an error term that decides when to step along the other axis.

The study meaning is this. Rasterizing a line means approximating a continuous path by grid decisions.

A useful closed check is this question. Why are incremental error updates useful in line rasterization?

The second idea in this lecture is Triangle Edge Rasterization.

Triangles are the core primitive in real time rendering. Edge functions or half space tests decide whether a sample lies inside the triangle. This allows the rasterizer to test coverage systematically.

Coverage is not final visibility. A covered sample becomes a fragment candidate. Depth testing, stencil testing, blending, and other operations still decide whether the framebuffer changes.

The study meaning is this. Triangle rasterization answers the question, which samples are inside this projected triangle?

A useful closed check is this question. What later stage can reject a fragment after triangle coverage succeeds?

The third idea in this lecture is Region Filling.

Region filling generalizes the idea of determining interior samples. The algorithm must identify connected areas or spans that should receive color. This connects rasterization to scan conversion and image space reasoning.

The practical issue is avoiding gaps, double fills, or inconsistent boundary handling. Small choices at edges can become visible artifacts when many primitives meet.

The study meaning is this. Filling is about turning boundary descriptions into consistent interior samples.

A useful closed check is this question. Why can inconsistent edge rules create cracks between adjacent primitives?

The fourth idea in this lecture is Scanline Based Triangle Rasterization.

Scanline rasterization processes horizontal rows of the image. For each row that crosses a triangle, the algorithm finds the covered interval and fills the span. Attribute interpolation can be updated along edges and across each span.

The method is intuitive because it follows the memory layout of many images. It also demonstrates why interpolation is tied to rasterization, once you know a sample location inside the primitive, you can interpolate depth, color, normals, or texture coordinates.

The study meaning is this. Each scanline asks, where does this triangle enter and leave this row?

A useful closed check is this question. Which attributes might be interpolated while filling triangle spans?

The fifth idea in this lecture is Tile Based Triangle Rasterization.

Tile or block based approaches group pixels into small regions. This can improve locality and parallel work distribution. Modern GPUs often reason in blocks or tiles internally because rendering is massively parallel.

For the exam, connect this to efficiency. The mathematical goal is still coverage, but the implementation is organized to use hardware resources well.

The study meaning is this. Block based rasterization keeps the same coverage problem but changes the work organization.

A useful closed check is this question. Why is block organization attractive for parallel graphics hardware?
